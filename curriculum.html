Level 1

Foundation

Weeks 1–4 · For AI newcomers (Placement score 0–7) · Earns: AI Aware Certificate

Learning Objectives

  • Explain in plain language how large language models generate text
  • Distinguish between what AI can and cannot do reliably — including the difference between the core model and its connected tools
  • Identify the three most common misconceptions about AI in business

Teaching Content 45 min

1. The Prediction Machine

LLMs predict the most statistically likely next token, thousands of times in sequence. They do not "know" things, search databases, or reason like humans. The core model is a pattern-matching engine trained on massive text data.

Live demo: Show the same prompt producing different outputs to illustrate probabilistic generation. Ask the AI "What is the capital of France?" (reliable — highly reinforced pattern) vs. "What was the closing price of Apple stock yesterday?" (unreliable — the model has no real-time data without tools).

Key distinction to teach: Many modern AI tools (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot) now have web browsing, code execution, and file-reading capabilities. These are tools connected to the model, not the model itself. The model can call these tools to get real information — but it can still misinterpret, selectively quote, or fabricate details even when the correct source is available. Retrieval does not equal comprehension.

2. What AI Is Good At vs. Bad At

Strong at: Summarizing long documents, drafting text in specified formats, translating between common languages, pattern recognition in text, brainstorming and idea generation, explaining concepts, code generation for common patterns.

Weak at: Precise calculations (though code execution tools help), niche domain facts without verification, anything requiring real-world judgment, predicting future events, understanding organizational context, distinguishing between what is true and what merely sounds true.

Exercise: Show 5 examples of each. Let participants predict which category each will fall into before revealing the result.

3. The Three Misconceptions

Misconception 1: "AI is always right" — It is confident, not correct. The model produces text that sounds authoritative regardless of whether it is accurate. Confidence of tone has zero correlation with factual accuracy.

Misconception 2: "AI understands my business" — It has patterns from training data, not context about your organization, your clients, your market position, or your constraints. Every AI output is generic until you add your context.

Misconception 3: "AI replaces thinking" — It augments thinking. The moment you stop evaluating AI output is the moment you become liable for whatever it gets wrong. AI shifts the work from creation to evaluation — it does not eliminate the work.

4. The Model + Tools Architecture

Modern AI systems are not monolithic — they are a core language model connected to tools:

  • Web search: Can retrieve current information, but the model can misread or misquote what it finds
  • Code execution: Can run calculations accurately, but the model may set up the wrong calculation
  • File reading: Can analyze your documents, but may miss nuance or context
  • Image generation/analysis: Can create and describe images, but makes errors in text, counting, and spatial relationships

The key lesson: Tools improve the inputs available to the model. They do not guarantee the outputs. A model with web access can still cite a real article and misrepresent what it says.

Hands-On Exercise 45 min

Participants open ChatGPT, Claude, or Copilot and complete these 6 tasks. For each task, record what happened and rate the AI's performance (Accurate / Partially accurate / Wrong).

  1. Summarization test: Give the AI a provided 2-page business document. Rate the summary: did it capture the key points? Did it add anything not in the original?
  2. Domain knowledge test: Ask the AI a factual question about your specific industry that you already know the answer to. Was it correct? Was it specific or generic?
  3. Consistency test: Ask the same question phrased two different ways. Compare the answers. Are they consistent? If not, what changed?
  4. Current information test: Ask the AI about something that happened this week in your industry. Does it use web search? If so, does it cite the source correctly? Verify the citation.
  5. Calculation test: Give the AI a 4-step business math problem (e.g., "If revenue is $2.3M and grows 12% YoY for 3 years, what is the Year 3 revenue?"). Check every step of the work.
  6. Competitive knowledge test: Ask the AI to list the top 5 competitors in your specific market niche. Verify: are these real companies? Are they actually competitors? Is the list complete or missing obvious ones?

Group Discussion 30 min

  • What surprised you most — where AI exceeded expectations, or where it failed?
  • Did the AI ever get something wrong with total confidence? How did it feel to read that?
  • How would you explain AI's capabilities and limits to a skeptical colleague in 2 minutes?
  • Where in your daily work might AI save time? Where would you not trust it?

Learning Objectives

  • Define AI hallucination and explain why it occurs even in models with web access
  • Identify 5 common hallucination patterns in business contexts
  • Apply source verification to any AI-generated claim

Teaching Content 45 min

1. What Is Hallucination?

The model generates plausible-sounding content that is factually wrong. It is not lying — it has no concept of truth. It is pattern-matching, and sometimes the pattern produces fiction that reads exactly like fact.

Critical point: Hallucination is not a bug that will be fixed — it is an inherent property of how language models generate text. Models with web access hallucinate less frequently but still do it, especially when they misquote sources, combine facts from different contexts, or fill gaps with plausible-sounding fabrications.

2. The 5 Hallucination Patterns
Pattern 1: Fabricated Statistics

The model invents numbers that sound reasonable: "The global CRM market grew 18.4% in 2025." The percentage sounds specific and authoritative. It may be completely fabricated. Even with web access, the model may pull a real report title but invent the specific figure.

How to catch: Any specific number needs a primary source. If you cannot find the exact number in the exact source claimed, it does not exist.

Pattern 2: False Citations

The model references papers, articles, reports, or regulations that do not exist — or cites real sources that do not actually say what the model claims they say. Example: "According to McKinsey's 2025 Digital Trust Report..." — the report may be real, but the specific claim attributed to it may not appear anywhere in it.

How to catch: Open the cited source. Read the relevant section. Does it actually say what the AI claims? Often it does not.

Pattern 3: Confident Extrapolation

The model extends trends beyond what data supports: "At current growth rates, the company will reach $50M revenue by 2028." The model treats a pattern as a certainty, ignoring market saturation, competition, and economic cycles.

How to catch: Question any forward projection. What assumptions is it based on? Are those assumptions stated? Would a domain expert make the same projection?

Pattern 4: Entity Confusion

The model mixes up companies, people, or products with similar names. Example: confusing Mercury Financial (a credit card company) with Mercury (a banking platform), or attributing one CEO's quote to another.

How to catch: Verify every proper noun. Does this company/person/product actually do what the model claims?

Pattern 5: Temporal Errors

The model applies old information to current questions, or invents future events. Example: citing a regulation that was proposed but never passed, or describing a product feature that was announced but later cancelled.

How to catch: Check dates. Is this information current? Has the situation changed since the model's training data or the article it cited?

3. Real-World Case Studies

Walk through 5 documented incidents:

  1. Legal: A lawyer used ChatGPT to draft a brief that cited 6 court cases. None of them existed. The lawyer was sanctioned by the court.
  2. Finance: An AI-generated investment memo included fabricated revenue figures for a competitor, leading to a flawed competitive analysis that reached the investment committee.
  3. Marketing: An AI produced a competitive analysis with made-up market share percentages, attributed to a Gartner report that did exist but did not contain those numbers.
  4. Healthcare: An AI medical summary described incorrect drug interactions that could have led to patient harm if not caught by a pharmacist.
  5. Legal/Regulatory: An AI-generated compliance memo cited a regulation that had been proposed in 2023 but was never enacted, advising the company to comply with a non-existent rule.
4. The Source Verification Habit

One rule that prevents most hallucination damage:

Before using any AI-generated fact in your work: Can I find this specific claim in a primary source? If I cannot, it does not go in my deliverable. No exceptions.

This takes 30 seconds per claim for common facts (a quick search) and 2-5 minutes for specific statistics or citations. It is the single highest-ROI habit in AI-assisted work.

Hands-On Exercise: The Hallucination Hunt 45 min

Participants receive 10 AI-generated paragraphs on business topics. Each paragraph contains 0-3 hallucinated claims mixed with accurate information. The challenge is to separate fact from fiction.

Instructions:

  1. Read each paragraph carefully
  2. Flag every claim you suspect is hallucinated (highlight or underline)
  3. Use the provided reference materials to verify or debunk each flagged claim
  4. For each flag, write: "Confirmed hallucination" or "False alarm — verified as accurate"

Scoring:

  • Correct flag (caught a real hallucination): +1 point
  • Missed hallucination (did not flag a fabricated claim): -1 point
  • False alarm (flagged something that was actually true): -0.5 points

Target score: 7+ out of a possible 12 on your first attempt. By the end of the bootcamp, you should score 10+.

Group Discussion 30 min

  • Which hallucination patterns were hardest to catch? Why?
  • Did anyone flag something as a hallucination that turned out to be true? What made it look suspicious?
  • In your professional domain, what would be the consequences of an undetected hallucination reaching a client or a decision-maker?
  • What verification habits can you realistically build into your daily workflow without adding significant time?

Teaching Content 45 min

The 5-Point AI Output Evaluation Framework

This is the core methodology of the entire bootcamp. Every session from here forward builds on this framework.

1
Source Verification

Can I trace this claim to a primary source?

Verified (source found, matches) · Unverified (no source found) · Contradicted (source found, disagrees)

2
Logical Consistency

Does the output contradict itself? Do the numbers add up? Does the conclusion follow from the evidence?

Consistent · Minor gaps · Contradictory

3
Domain Plausibility

Based on my professional knowledge, does this make sense for my industry, market, and context?

Plausible · Questionable · Implausible

4
Statistical Validity

Are the numbers reasonable? Are percentages, growth rates, and comparisons mathematically sound?

Valid · Suspect · Invalid

5
Recency & Relevance

Is this information current enough for my purpose? Could it be outdated even if the model has web access?

Current · Possibly outdated · Likely outdated

Overall Confidence Rating
High Confidence

All 5 criteria pass. Safe to use with light editing. Still spot-check one key claim.

Medium Confidence

3-4 criteria pass. Use with caveats. Verify the failed criteria before presenting to stakeholders.

Low Confidence

2 or fewer pass. Do not use without substantial independent research. The AI output is a starting point, not a deliverable.

Hands-On Exercise 45 min

Participants receive 5 AI-generated business documents. For each:

  1. Complete the 5-point evaluation framework worksheet
  2. Assign a confidence rating (High / Medium / Low)
  3. Write a 2-sentence recommendation: use as-is / use with caveats / do not use

The 5 documents:

  • A market analysis for a SaaS product entering the healthcare sector
  • A risk assessment for opening a new office in Singapore
  • A project proposal summary for an IT infrastructure upgrade
  • A customer insight report based on survey data
  • A draft company policy on remote work

Group Discussion 30 min

  • Share your confidence ratings. Where did the group agree? Where did you disagree?
  • Which of the 5 criteria was hardest to evaluate? Why?
  • How long did the evaluation take per document? Is this realistic for daily use?
  • What shortcuts can you take for low-stakes work vs. high-stakes work?

Learning Objectives

  • Decompose complex problems into structured AI-assisted steps

Teaching Content 45 min

Four Prompt Frameworks
1. BAB (Before-After-Bridge)

For: Problem-solving, process improvement, gap analysis

Structure: "Here is the current situation [Before]. Here is where we need to be [After]. Help me build the plan to get there [Bridge]."

Example: "Before: Our customer onboarding takes 14 days and we lose 30% of new signups before completion. After: Onboarding completed in 5 days with less than 10% drop-off. Bridge: What process changes, automations, and communication improvements would close this gap?"

2. COAST (Context-Objective-Actions-Scenario-Task)

For: Strategic planning, scenario analysis, market entry decisions

Structure: Provide full context, state the objective, list possible actions, describe scenario constraints, assign the task.

Example: "Context: We are a 200-person B2B SaaS company with $15M ARR, primarily serving North American clients. Objective: Evaluate entering the European market. Actions we're considering: direct sales, partnerships, or acquisition. Scenario: We have $3M to invest, 12-month timeline, and must be GDPR compliant. Task: Build a decision framework comparing these three approaches."

3. Chain-of-Thought

For: Analytical problems, financial analysis, root cause analysis

Structure: "Think through this step by step" or "Show your reasoning at each stage."

Example: "Our Q3 revenue came in 12% below forecast. Walk me through step by step: what are the possible drivers of a revenue miss of this size? For each driver, what data would I look at to confirm or rule it out?"

4. Decomposition

For: Complex business cases, due diligence, competitive analysis

Structure: Break the big question into 5-7 smaller questions. Ask AI each one separately. Synthesize the answers yourself.

Example: Instead of "Should we acquire CompetitorX?" → Break into: What is their revenue and growth trajectory? What is their technology stack and IP? What is the cultural fit risk? What are the regulatory implications? What would integration cost? What synergies are realistic? Ask each separately, evaluate each answer, synthesize your own recommendation.

When NOT to Use AI
  • Legal liability: Decisions with legal consequences unless output is reviewed by qualified counsel
  • Confidential data: Anything requiring data you cannot share with the tool (check your company's AI policy and the tool's data handling terms)
  • Error cost exceeds time saved: When the business cost of an undetected AI error is greater than the time you would save
  • Cannot evaluate output: When you do not have the domain knowledge to judge whether the AI's answer is right or wrong
  • Emotional/relational decisions: Delivering difficult feedback, managing a team conflict, handling a sensitive client situation — AI can help you prepare, but the human delivery and judgment are irreplaceable

Hands-On Exercise 45 min

3 business problems, each requiring a different prompt framework. For each:

  1. Choose the right framework and explain why
  2. Write and execute the prompt
  3. Evaluate the output using the Session 3 framework
  4. Improve the output with a follow-up prompt

The problems:

  • Problem 1 (BAB): Your team's customer support response time averages 4 hours. The target is under 1 hour. Build a reduction plan.
  • Problem 2 (COAST): Your company is considering launching a subscription tier for a product currently sold as a one-time purchase. Build a scenario analysis.
  • Problem 3 (Decomposition): Your CEO wants to know whether opening a second office in another city makes sense. Break into sub-questions and research each.

Level 2

Practitioner

Weeks 5–8 · For AI-literate professionals (Placement score 8–14) · Earns: AI Practitioner Certificate

Learning Objectives

  • Apply a 4-level decision framework: Trust / Verify / Modify / Reject
  • Identify the 3 factors that determine the right override level (stakes, reversibility, expertise)
  • Practice making override decisions under time pressure

Teaching Content 45 min

The Override Decision Tree

Every AI output demands one of four responses:

Trust

Use as-is with light editing. When: all 5 evaluation criteria pass, stakes are low, output aligns with domain knowledge.

Verify

Use after checking specific claims. When: 3-4 criteria pass, 1-2 claims need source verification. Most business AI use should land here.

Modify

Keep the structure, replace specific content. When: framework is sound but facts, numbers, or recommendations need correction based on your expertise.

Reject

Do not use. Start over manually. When: fundamental logic flaws, critical factual errors, or misunderstood problem.

The 3 Factors
  • Stakes: What is the cost of an undetected error? Low = internal draft. High = board presentation, legal filing, client commitment.
  • Reversibility: Can the decision be undone? Reversible = internal process change. Irreversible = public statement, contract clause, hiring decision.
  • Your expertise: How well can YOU evaluate this specific output? High = your core domain. Low = unfamiliar territory.

Rule: When stakes are high, reversibility is low, or expertise is limited — move UP the override ladder (from Trust toward Reject).

Hands-On Exercise: Speed Override Drill 45 min

12 AI outputs in rapid succession (one every 3-4 minutes). For each: read (1 min), assign Trust/Verify/Modify/Reject (30 sec), justify in one sentence (1 min), compare with facilitator's answer (1-2 min).

Outputs span: marketing email, financial projection, legal clause, hiring recommendation, customer complaint response, risk assessment, meeting summary, product roadmap suggestion, competitive analysis, policy draft, data interpretation, strategic recommendation.

Teaching Content 45 min

The Red Team Framework — 6 Attack Vectors
1. Source Attack

Are the cited sources real? Current? The best sources for this claim?

2. Logic Attack

Does the conclusion follow from the evidence? False causation? Circular reasoning?

3. Completeness Attack

What is missing? Which perspectives, data, risks, or stakeholders are absent?

4. Bias Attack

Does the output favor one side? Reflect training biases? Tell the user what they want to hear?

5. Context Attack

Does it account for your specific industry, geography, company size, culture, timing?

6. Adversarial Stress Test

If a hostile critic read this, what would they attack first? What is the weakest claim?

The Communication Framework
  1. Lead with the decision, not the tool. "Our analysis indicates X" — not "The AI says X."
  2. Disclose the method when relevant. "We used AI-assisted analysis, validated against [sources]."

Level 3

Advanced

Weeks 9–12 · For AI-experienced leaders (Placement score 15–20) · Earns: AI Critical Thinker Certificate

Teaching Content 45 min

The Workflow Architecture Method
  1. Map the current process — every step, decision point, handoff
  2. Classify each step: AI-suitable (high volume, pattern-based, low stakes) · Human-required (high stakes, novel, ethical) · AI-assisted, human-decided (AI prepares, human decides)
  3. Insert checkpoints: Who reviews, what they check, how long they have, what triggers escalation
  4. Build feedback loops: How does the reviewer flag errors back to improve the process?
The 3 Workflow Design Mistakes
  • Over-automation: Automating decisions that need human judgment because "the AI is usually right"
  • Rubber-stamp checkpoints: A human "reviews" but never actually changes anything — compliance theater
  • Missing escalation paths: No process for when AI produces something the reviewer cannot evaluate

Teaching Content 45 min

7 Components of a Practical AI Use Policy
  1. Approved tools and purposes
  2. Data classification matrix — public (any tool), internal (enterprise tools only), confidential (never external AI)
  3. Human review requirements — low stakes (self-review), medium (peer), high (senior + documentation)
  4. Prohibited use cases — where AI must not be used, period
  5. Disclosure requirements — when to tell clients/regulators AI was used
  6. Incident reporting — what to do when AI errors reach clients or cause impact
  7. Training requirements — who must complete AI training before using tools for work
