Case Study 1 Pre-work for Session 2

When AI Got the Numbers Wrong — How a Financial Services Firm Caught a $2M Error

Industry: Financial ServicesPattern: Fabricated StatisticsRead time: 8 min

What Happened

In late 2024, a mid-sized asset management firm began using an AI assistant to draft quarterly investment reports for institutional clients. The process seemed straightforward: an analyst would feed the AI portfolio data, market summaries, and performance benchmarks, and the AI would produce a polished 12-page report draft.

For three quarters, the process worked well. Reports were faster to produce, consistently formatted, and clients praised the improved clarity. The analyst reviewed each report before sending, scanning for tone, structure, and obvious errors.

In Q3 2025, the AI produced a report for a $180M pension fund client that included this paragraph:

"The portfolio's exposure to emerging market debt was reduced from 14.2% to 8.7% during the quarter, consistent with the committee's risk reduction mandate. This rebalancing captured $2.1M in realized gains from the EM debt positions, contributing to the portfolio's overall 3.8% quarterly return."

The analyst read through the report during a busy Friday afternoon. The numbers looked reasonable. The language matched previous reports. She approved it for distribution.

What Went Wrong

A junior analyst on the team, reviewing the report the following Monday for a different purpose, noticed the $2.1M gain figure. He checked the actual trade records. The EM debt rebalancing had generated $340K in realized gains — not $2.1M. The AI had fabricated the specific dollar amount while keeping everything else about the paragraph accurate.

Further investigation revealed:

  • The EM debt allocation reduction was accurate (14.2% to 8.7%)
  • The quarterly return of 3.8% was accurate
  • The $2.1M figure was completely fabricated — it was not in any source data
  • The figure was plausible enough to pass a casual review (it was roughly proportional to the allocation size)

The report had already been sent to the pension fund's investment committee. The firm had to issue a correction within 48 hours.

Why the Human Missed It

Three factors conspired:

  1. Pattern blindness: After three successful quarters, the analyst had developed trust in the AI's output. Her review became a skim rather than a verification.
  2. Plausibility trap: The fabricated number was in the right ballpark — not wildly high or low. Hallucinated statistics are usually plausible, which is precisely what makes them dangerous.
  3. Context camouflage: The fabricated number was embedded in a paragraph where everything else was accurate. The surrounding accuracy created a false sense of reliability for the one false claim.

The Override Protocol That Should Have Existed

  • Rule 1: Every specific dollar amount, percentage, and date in AI-generated financial reports must be traced to a source record before approval. No exceptions.
  • Rule 2: Reviewer must verify at least 3 specific claims per page against source data — not just read for flow.
  • Rule 3: Any report going to an external client requires a second reviewer who did not participate in drafting.

The Aftermath

The correction was issued. The client was understanding but requested that all future reports include a "data verification statement" confirming that specific figures had been checked against source records. The firm implemented a mandatory verification checklist for all AI-assisted client deliverables. The total cost: approximately 120 hours of remediation work, one difficult client conversation, and a revised workflow that added 45 minutes per report — still faster than the fully manual process, but with a verification checkpoint that would have caught the error.

Discussion Questions

  1. At what point did the analyst's review process break down? Was it a process failure or a human psychology failure?
  2. The fabricated number was "plausible." How do you verify numbers that look reasonable but might be fabricated?
  3. The firm added 45 minutes per report for verification. Is this a reasonable trade-off? How would you decide how much verification time is appropriate?
  4. Could this happen in your work? What would the consequences be?
Case Study 2 Pre-work for Session 2

The Citation That Didn't Exist — How to Verify AI Sources

Industry: LegalPattern: False CitationsRead time: 7 min

What Happened

In June 2023, New York attorney Steven Schwartz used ChatGPT to research case law for a personal injury lawsuit against the airline Avianca. The AI provided several relevant-sounding case citations, complete with court names, docket numbers, and brief summaries of each ruling.

Schwartz included six of these cases in his court filing. He later testified that he had asked ChatGPT whether the cases were real, and the AI confirmed that they were. He did not verify any of the cases in an actual legal database.

What Went Wrong

None of the six cases existed. Every citation was fabricated by the AI:

  • Varghese v. China Southern Airlines Co. Ltd., 925 F.3d 1339 (11th Cir. 2019) — fabricated
  • Shaboon v. Egyptair, 2013 IL App (1st) 111279-U — fabricated
  • Petersen v. Iran Air, 905 F. Supp 2d 121 (D.D.C. 2012) — fabricated

The citations followed real legal citation format perfectly. They included plausible court names, realistic docket numbers, and summaries that sounded like actual judicial reasoning. Each fabricated case conveniently supported the attorney's argument.

When the opposing counsel could not find the cases, the judge ordered Schwartz to verify them. He went back to ChatGPT and asked if they were real. ChatGPT confirmed they were. He submitted this confirmation to the court.

Why This Matters for Every Professional

This is not a legal problem — it is a verification problem that applies to every field:

  • AI generates citations in the correct format for any field: legal cases, academic papers, financial reports, regulatory documents, industry standards
  • Asking the AI to verify its own output is not verification. The model does not have a separate fact-checking system — it will generate a confident confirmation using the same pattern-matching that created the fabrication
  • Plausible formatting creates false credibility. A properly formatted citation looks authoritative regardless of whether the underlying source exists

The Verification Protocol

  1. Never ask the AI to verify its own claims. This is asking the source of the error to confirm there is no error. It will almost always confirm.
  2. Go to the primary source. For legal cases: Westlaw, LexisNexis, court databases. For academic papers: Google Scholar, the journal's website. For financial data: SEC filings, company investor relations. For industry reports: the publisher's website.
  3. Verify the specific claim, not just the source. A real source may exist but not say what the AI claims it says. Open the document and read the relevant section.
  4. If you cannot find it, it probably does not exist. AI does not have access to secret sources. If a citation cannot be found through standard research channels, treat it as fabricated until proven otherwise.

The Consequences

Judge P. Kevin Castel sanctioned Schwartz and his firm $5,000, called the filing an "unprecedented circumstance" of submitting fabricated cases, and required them to notify every judge whose name appeared in the fake citations. The case became international news and a landmark example of AI misuse in professional settings.

Discussion Questions

  1. Schwartz asked ChatGPT if the cases were real and it said yes. Why doesn't this count as verification? What would proper verification look like?
  2. In your field, what types of citations or references might AI fabricate? How would you check them?
  3. The attorney later said he "did not know ChatGPT could make things up." Is this a reasonable defense in 2026? What is a professional's responsibility to understand their tools?
  4. Design a 3-step verification rule for your work: what would you always check before using AI-generated references?
Case Study 3 Pre-work for Session 3

The Confident Forecast — When AI Extrapolated a Trend That Didn't Exist

Industry: SaaS / TechnologyPattern: Confident ExtrapolationRead time: 7 min

What Happened

A Series B SaaS startup used AI to prepare investor materials for their Series C fundraise. The AI was given 18 months of revenue data showing strong growth: from $1.2M ARR to $4.8M ARR — a 4x increase in 18 months.

The AI generated a forecast projecting this growth rate forward: $19.2M ARR by the end of the following year, $76.8M by year after. The projection was presented in a clean chart with clear labels and a confident narrative: "Based on current trajectory, the company is on track to achieve $77M ARR within 36 months."

What Went Wrong

The AI treated the historical growth rate as a constant and extrapolated it linearly (actually exponentially). It did not account for:

  • Market saturation: The company had captured most of the early-adopter segment. Growth from mainstream customers would be slower and more expensive.
  • Churn acceleration: As the customer base grew, churn began increasing — a signal the AI ignored because the prompt focused on revenue, not retention.
  • Sales cycle lengthening: Enterprise deals were taking 3x longer to close than the SMB deals that drove early growth.
  • Competitive response: Two well-funded competitors had entered the market in the previous quarter.

The actual result 12 months later: $8.1M ARR — respectable growth, but less than half of the AI's $19.2M projection. The company had shared the AI's forecast with potential investors.

Why the Human Missed It

The CEO wanted the projection to be true. The AI produced exactly the narrative the CEO hoped to tell. This is the confirmation bias trap: AI outputs that align with what we want to hear receive less scrutiny than outputs that challenge us.

A CFO reviewing the same data would have asked: "What is the basis for assuming this growth rate continues? What are the leading indicators suggesting it will or will not?" The AI was never asked these questions.

The Evaluation Framework Applied

  • Source Verification: The historical data was accurate. But a projection is not a fact — it is a model of the future built on assumptions.
  • Logical Consistency: The math was correct given the assumption. The assumption itself was flawed.
  • Domain Plausibility: Any experienced SaaS operator knows that early-stage growth rates do not sustain. This should have been the first red flag.
  • Statistical Validity: Extrapolating 18 months of data to predict 36 months forward violates basic forecasting principles. The confidence interval should have been enormous.
  • Overall: Low confidence. The data was real, but the projection was a fantasy built on an unstated assumption that nothing would change.

Discussion Questions

  1. How would you evaluate an AI-generated forecast differently from an AI-generated summary of historical facts?
  2. The CEO wanted the numbers to be true. How do you protect against confirmation bias when using AI?
  3. What questions should you always ask when AI produces a forward-looking projection?
  4. If you were the CFO reviewing this, what would you have asked for before sharing with investors?
Case Study 4Pre-work for Session 4

The Perfect Prompt, The Wrong Answer — Why Better Prompting Doesn't Fix Factual Errors

Industry: Management ConsultingPattern: Prompt Over-RelianceRead time: 6 min

What Happened

A management consultant, proud of their prompt engineering skills, used a carefully structured COAST framework prompt to generate a competitive analysis for a client in the industrial automation sector. The prompt was exemplary: clear context, specific objective, defined scenario constraints, and a precise task.

The AI produced a beautifully structured analysis with a clear recommendation, supporting data, and a risk assessment. The consultant was satisfied — the prompt had worked exactly as designed. She delivered the analysis to the client with minor formatting edits.

What Went Wrong

The analysis named five competitors and attributed specific product capabilities, pricing tiers, and market positions to each. Three of the five descriptions were materially wrong:

  • Competitor A's "new IoT platform" did not exist — it was a product from a similarly-named company in a different industry
  • Competitor B's pricing was described as "$45K per unit" — the actual price was $12K, a difference that completely changed the competitive positioning
  • Competitor C had been acquired 8 months earlier and no longer operated as an independent company

The client's VP of Sales knew the competitive landscape intimately and immediately flagged the errors, damaging the consulting firm's credibility.

The Lesson

Prompt engineering improves the structure and relevance of AI output. It does not improve factual accuracy. A perfectly prompted AI can produce a beautifully organized collection of fabrications. The evaluation framework applies regardless of how good your prompt was.

Discussion Questions

  1. The consultant's prompt was excellent. Where did the process fail?
  2. How do you maintain healthy skepticism about AI output even when your prompt produced exactly what you asked for?
  3. The client caught the errors — what would have happened if the client was less knowledgeable about the competitive landscape?
Case Study 5Pre-work for Session 5

Trust, Then Regret — When a Manager Should Have Rejected an AI Recommendation

Industry: Human ResourcesPattern: Failed Override DecisionRead time: 7 min

What Happened

An HR director at a 500-person technology company used an AI tool to analyze employee engagement survey data and recommend interventions. The AI analyzed 18 months of quarterly survey data across 12 teams and produced a detailed report with specific recommendations for each team.

One recommendation stood out: "Team Gamma shows a 34% decline in engagement scores correlated with the transition to hybrid work. Recommendation: require Team Gamma to return to full-time in-office work to restore engagement levels."

The HR director forwarded the recommendation to the VP of Engineering, who implemented a mandatory return-to-office policy for Team Gamma.

What Went Wrong

Within 6 weeks of the policy change:

  • 3 of Team Gamma's 11 engineers resigned, citing the loss of flexibility as the primary reason
  • Two others began actively interviewing, as reported by their managers
  • The actual cause of the engagement decline was not hybrid work — it was a contentious project reassignment 6 months earlier that the survey data could not capture directly

The AI had found a correlation (engagement declined during the same period as hybrid adoption) and presented it as a causal recommendation. The HR director had the domain knowledge to question this — she knew about the project reassignment — but deferred to the AI's "data-driven" analysis.

The Override Decision That Should Have Been Made

  • Stakes: High — directly affects employee retention and team morale
  • Reversibility: Low — once people resign, they do not come back
  • Expertise: The HR director had context the AI did not
  • Correct decision: REJECT the AI recommendation. Investigate the actual cause of engagement decline using conversations and context, not just survey correlation.

Discussion Questions

  1. The HR director had the context to question the recommendation but didn't. Why do professionals defer to AI on decisions in their own domain?
  2. How would you apply the Override Decision Tree (Trust/Verify/Modify/Reject) to this situation?
  3. What is the difference between correlation and causation in AI outputs? How do you catch the difference?
  4. Design a 3-step check for any AI recommendation that involves people decisions.
Case Study 6Pre-work for Session 6

The Strategy No One Attacked — How Groupthink Meets AI Confidence

Industry: Retail / E-commercePattern: Missing Adversarial ReviewRead time: 6 min

What Happened

A mid-sized e-commerce company used AI to develop a market expansion strategy for Southeast Asia. The AI produced a comprehensive 15-page analysis recommending Vietnam as the priority market, with detailed supporting arguments: growing middle class, high smartphone penetration, favorable regulatory environment, and limited established competition.

The strategy team reviewed the document, found it well-structured and convincing, and presented it to the board. The board approved a $4M investment in Vietnam market entry.

What Went Wrong

Six months into execution, the team discovered:

  • The "limited competition" claim was wrong — two major regional players had entered Vietnam in the previous 9 months, a fact the AI missed because its analysis relied on outdated competitive data
  • The regulatory environment had changed significantly — new foreign e-commerce regulations had been proposed (and were later enacted) that required local entity establishment and data localization
  • The "growing middle class" data was accurate but the purchasing power parity analysis was wrong — the AI used GDP per capita rather than disposable income, overstating the addressable market by approximately 3x
  • Thailand, which the AI had ranked third, was actually a better fit based on the company's existing logistics partnerships

The fundamental problem: nobody played Red Team. The analysis was well-written and data-rich, and the team accepted it because it confirmed a direction they were already leaning toward.

Discussion Questions

  1. Apply the 6 Red Team attack vectors to the AI's Vietnam recommendation. What would each vector have found?
  2. Why is it psychologically harder to attack a well-written, data-rich analysis than a clearly weak one?
  3. How could the team have structured their review process to ensure adversarial thinking?
  4. What is the cost of NOT doing a Red Team review on a $4M strategic decision?
Case Study 7Pre-work for Session 7

The Compliance Checklist That Missed the Law — Domain Expertise vs. AI Generalism

Industry: HealthcarePattern: Domain-Specific FailureRead time: 6 min

What Happened

A healthcare technology startup used AI to generate a HIPAA compliance checklist for their new patient data platform. The AI produced a 42-point checklist that covered the major HIPAA requirements: access controls, encryption, audit logging, breach notification, Business Associate Agreements, and privacy notices.

The checklist looked comprehensive. The CTO used it as the basis for the company's compliance program, checking off items as the engineering team implemented each one.

What Went Wrong

During a compliance audit 8 months later, the auditor identified several critical gaps:

  • The checklist did not address the HIPAA Minimum Necessary Standard — the requirement that access to patient data be limited to the minimum necessary for a specific purpose. The platform granted all authenticated users access to all patient records.
  • The checklist referenced the HIPAA Omnibus Rule of 2013 but missed updates from subsequent OCR guidance on cloud computing and telehealth data handling — directly relevant to a cloud-based platform.
  • The checklist included a requirement for "annual risk assessment" but described a process that did not meet the specific methodology requirements of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, which OCR uses as a benchmark.
  • Several state-specific healthcare privacy laws (California CMIA, New York SHIELD Act) were not mentioned at all.

The AI had produced a checklist that was approximately 70% correct — enough to create a false sense of compliance while leaving significant legal exposure.

Discussion Questions

  1. The AI got 70% of the compliance requirements right. Why is 70% compliance actually more dangerous than 0% compliance?
  2. What domain-specific knowledge did the auditor have that the AI lacked?
  3. For your professional domain, what are the "Minimum Necessary Standards" — the requirements that sound simple but have specific interpretations that only practitioners know?
  4. How would you use AI to assist with compliance work without falling into this trap?
Case Study 8Pre-work for Session 8

"The AI Says" — How a VP Lost Board Confidence by Not Owning the Recommendation

Industry: ManufacturingPattern: Communication FailureRead time: 5 min

What Happened

A VP of Operations at a manufacturing company used AI to analyze supply chain data and develop a recommendation for reshoring a critical component from Southeast Asia to Mexico. The AI produced a detailed cost-benefit analysis, risk assessment, and implementation timeline.

During the board presentation, the VP presented the findings beginning with: "Our AI analysis recommends reshoring to Mexico." When a board member asked about the tariff assumptions, the VP replied: "That's what the AI's model showed." When another board member challenged the labor cost projections, the VP said: "The AI used current market data."

What Went Wrong

The board lost confidence — not in the analysis, but in the VP. The perception: this executive did not understand their own recommendation. They were presenting an AI's output, not their own strategic judgment.

The board tabled the decision and requested the VP return with "an analysis you can defend yourself, using whatever tools you find appropriate." The reshoring project was delayed by two months.

What Should Have Happened

  • Own the recommendation: "Our analysis indicates that reshoring to Mexico reduces supply chain risk by 40% and total landed cost by 8%. Here's why..."
  • Be transparent about methodology: "We used AI-assisted analysis to model 15 scenarios, then validated the top 3 against supplier quotes and site visits."
  • Handle challenges with substance: "The tariff assumptions use current MFN rates plus a 10% buffer for policy risk. Here's what changes if tariffs increase by 25%..."
  • Never say "the AI says": You are the decision-maker. The AI is a tool. Executives who abdicate judgment to tools lose their mandate.

Discussion Questions

  1. What is the difference between "The AI recommends X" and "Our analysis indicates X"? Why does it matter?
  2. When should you disclose that AI was used? When should you not?
  3. How do you prepare to defend an AI-assisted recommendation under hostile questioning?
Case Study 9Pre-work for Session 9

The Automated Approval — How Removing a Human Checkpoint Cost $800K

Industry: InsurancePattern: Over-AutomationRead time: 7 min

What Happened

A regional insurance company implemented an AI system to process routine claims. The system worked well for straightforward claims — auto-approving clear-cut cases and flagging ambiguous ones for human review. After 6 months with a 97% accuracy rate, the operations team decided to expand the auto-approval threshold, removing the human checkpoint for claims under $25K that the AI rated as "high confidence."

What Went Wrong

Over the next 4 months, the expanded auto-approval system processed approximately 3,200 claims without human review. An internal audit found:

  • 47 claims had been overpaid by a total of $340K due to the AI misinterpreting policy endorsements and riders
  • 12 claims that should have been flagged for fraud investigation were auto-approved, resulting in an estimated $460K in fraudulent payouts
  • The AI had developed a pattern of approving claims from a specific geographic region at higher rates — later traced to a fraud ring that had figured out how to structure claims to pass the AI's checks

Total cost: approximately $800K in overpayments and fraud, plus regulatory scrutiny from the state insurance commissioner.

The Workflow Design Failure

The original workflow had three layers: AI pre-screening, human review, and supervisor spot-checks. When the team removed the human review layer for "high confidence" claims, they eliminated the only checkpoint that could catch errors the AI couldn't detect in itself. The 97% accuracy rate was measured during the period when human review caught the 3% — without that catch, the error rate was actually higher, and the errors were systematically exploitable.

Discussion Questions

  1. The AI had a 97% accuracy rate. Why wasn't this good enough to remove human review?
  2. How did the fraud ring exploit the AI's patterns? What does this tell us about adversarial risk in automated systems?
  3. Design a workflow that keeps the AI's speed benefit while maintaining meaningful human oversight for claims under $25K.
Case Study 10Pre-work for Session 10

The Data Leak — When an Employee Pasted Client Data Into a Public AI Tool

Industry: Professional ServicesPattern: Governance FailureRead time: 6 min

What Happened

A senior consultant at an accounting firm was preparing a complex tax analysis for a Fortune 500 client. Under time pressure, she pasted 14 pages of the client's financial data — including revenue figures, executive compensation details, and pending acquisition information — into ChatGPT (public version) to help structure the analysis.

The AI produced an excellent first draft. The consultant finished the analysis 2 hours faster than usual. She did not think about the data implications until a colleague mentioned the firm's recently issued AI use policy the following week.

What Went Wrong

The firm's general counsel determined that the data submission likely violated:

  • The client's confidentiality agreement, which prohibited sharing financial data with third parties without written consent
  • The firm's own data handling policy
  • Potentially, SEC regulations regarding material non-public information (the pending acquisition data)

The firm was required to: notify the client, conduct an internal investigation, report to the relevant regulatory body, and implement remediation measures. The client relationship survived but required significant repair. The firm invested approximately $200K in policy development, training, and technology controls in the aftermath.

The Policy That Should Have Existed

At the time, the firm had no AI use policy. After the incident, they implemented:

  • Data classification matrix: Clear rules on what can be entered into which tools (public data → any tool; internal → approved enterprise tools only; client/confidential → never in external AI)
  • Approved tool list: Enterprise AI tools with data processing agreements replaced public tools for all client work
  • Mandatory training: All staff complete AI data handling training before accessing AI tools
  • Incident reporting: Clear process for reporting data handling mistakes without fear of immediate punishment (encouraging early reporting)

Discussion Questions

  1. The consultant was trying to do better work faster. How do you create policies that enable AI use while protecting data?
  2. What data from your work should NEVER be entered into a public AI tool? Where is the line?
  3. The incident reporting process was designed to encourage early reporting. Why is this important? What happens when organizations punish reporting?

Reading List

Recommended Reading by Level

Curated articles, papers, and books. None required — all optional for those who want to go deeper.

Level 1: Foundation

"On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" — Bender et al., 2021

The foundational paper on LLM limitations. Dense but essential for understanding why these models generate plausible-sounding nonsense. Read the abstract and Section 6.

"Sparks of Artificial General Intelligence" — Microsoft Research, 2023

The counter-argument: evidence of emergent capabilities in GPT-4. Read alongside "Stochastic Parrots" for a balanced view. Sections 1–3 are most relevant.

"What ChatGPT Can't Do" — Harvard Business Review, 2023

Accessible business-focused overview of AI limitations. Good starting point for anyone new to the topic.

Level 2: Practitioner

"Thinking, Fast and Slow" — Daniel Kahneman, 2011

The definitive work on cognitive biases and decision-making. Chapters on anchoring, confirmation bias, and overconfidence directly apply to AI-assisted decisions.

"Red Team: How to Succeed by Thinking Like the Enemy" — Micah Zenko, 2015

Practical guide to adversarial thinking from military and intelligence contexts. The Red Team methodology in Session 6 draws from this tradition.

"Human Compatible: AI and the Problem of Control" — Stuart Russell, 2019

AI safety from one of the field's founders. Chapters 4-7 on the alignment problem are directly relevant to human oversight design.

Level 3: Advanced

"The NIST AI Risk Management Framework" — NIST, 2023

The US government's framework for AI governance. Essential reading for anyone writing organizational AI policy. Focus on the GOVERN and MAP functions.

"The EU AI Act: A Summary" — Future of Life Institute, 2024

Overview of the world's first comprehensive AI regulation. Relevant for any organization with European operations or clients.

"Power and Prediction: The Disruptive Economics of AI" — Agrawal, Gans & Goldfarb, 2022

From the authors of "Prediction Machines." How AI changes decision-making in organizations. Directly relevant to workflow design and governance.
